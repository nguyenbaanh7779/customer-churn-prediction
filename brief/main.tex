\section*{The decision}
Each week the retailer can send a 10\% discount voucher to some of its customers. A voucher only pays off for a customer who was \emph{about to stop buying} and stays because of it. For a customer who would have bought anyway, it is simply money given away. So the question is not ``who buys a lot?'' but \textbf{``who should get a voucher, given a fixed weekly discount budget?''}

\section*{Our answer}
Every Monday, a statistical model gives each active customer a score: how likely they are to make \emph{no purchase in the next 90 days}. We send the voucher to the highest-scoring customers until the weekly discount budget is used up. With a budget of about \pounds7,700 of discounts per week, that is roughly \textbf{780 customers a week}.

In a simulation on 10 weeks the model had never seen (July--September 2011), this earns \textbf{\pounds8,700 of extra profit} per weekly group over the next 90 days. Picking the customers who have been silent the longest, with the same discount budget, earns \pounds5,800, so the model gives about \textbf{51\% more}. Sending the voucher to everyone costs \pounds33,000 in discounts and earns only \pounds4,400. These are simulated results, not measured sales.

\section*{How we count the money (simple version)}
We have no data on past vouchers or product costs, so the following are \textbf{assumptions}, tested in a pilot:

\begin{center}
\small\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.12}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.3cm}Y@{}}
\toprule
Voucher & 10\% off one order, worth the customer's usual order value $V$ \\
Customer about to leave & 30\% of them stay and buy because of the voucher \\
Customer who would buy anyway & 40\% of them use the voucher (we lose the 10\%) \\
Margin and cost & 40\% gross margin on an order; \pounds0.50 to contact one customer \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\textbf{Example, customer with $V=$ \pounds300.} If they were about to leave: 30\% $\times$ \pounds300 $\times$ (40\% margin $-$ 10\% discount) $-$ \pounds0.50 $=$ \textbf{+\pounds26.50}. If they would have bought anyway: $-$40\% $\times$ \pounds300 $\times$ 10\% $-$ \pounds0.50 $=$ \textbf{$-$\pounds12.50}. A good list has many customers of the first kind and few of the second. ``Extra profit'' below adds these numbers up over the customers we picked, using what really happened to them in the following 90 days.

\textbf{What this tells us.} (1) The model is what creates the value: with the same discount, it beats ``silent the longest'' in each of the 10 weeks. (2) Adding order size to the choice does not make more profit with the same discount; it reaches 38\% fewer customers for slightly less profit, so it is the lighter option when each contact is costly. (3) Voucher to everyone wastes most of the discount. In an earlier backtest over four seasons, it lost money at a 20\% discount in three of the four.

\section*{Who the offers go to}
On the list for 5 December 2011 (772 offers), the customer groups come from the RFM segmentation: \textbf{One-time recent} (346), \textbf{Developing} (230) and \textbf{Cooling} (188) get nearly all of the offers and the expected profit. \textbf{Champions and Loyal} get only 7 offers: they mostly buy anyway, and in the test weeks a voucher to a Champion lost about \pounds15 per contact, to a Loyal customer about nothing.

\section*{Can we trust it? What to check}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=0.1em,topsep=0.2em]
\item \textbf{The model is useful but not perfect.} On the 10 test weeks, the 10\% of customers it ranked most at risk left 1.9 times as often as the average customer.
\item \textbf{Seasons.} About 61\% of customers stop buying in December--January but only 40\% in July--September, and the model does not know the calendar. Its ranking stays useful; its exact risk numbers are too low in winter and too high in summer.
\item \textbf{One-time buyers} are 13\% of customers but 44\% of the offer list, and the model is less accurate for them. Pilot results should be reported separately for this group.
\item \textbf{Big orders.} About 7\% of offers go to customers with average orders above \pounds1,000. Capping order value at \pounds500 lowers the profit of the ``balanced'' list by about a fifth.
\item \textbf{All the money figures depend on the assumptions above.} If the real margin is below about 20\%, the offers do not pay back. Real product margins should replace our 40\%.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Next step: a small pilot}
Run the rule for 6--8 weeks and keep 20\% of the selected customers as a no-offer control group. Follow every customer for 90 days and compare purchases and profit after discounts between the two groups, separately for one-time and repeat buyers. Expand only if the offer group clearly earns more, the discount stays within budget, and the real response is close to our assumptions (30\% and 40\%).
