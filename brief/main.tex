\section{The Opportunity}
Retaining an existing customer costs far less than acquiring a new one, and revenue is highly concentrated: the \textbf{top 5\% of customers generate 52\% of revenue}, and the top 10\% generate 64\%. Protecting this base from churn is where retention spend has the most leverage.

\end{center}

\section{What We Built}
A weekly churn early-warning score for every active customer, built on point-in-time RFM and purchase-behavior features and a Logistic Regression classifier. On a held-out 10-week test period (2,907 customers, 40.1\% churned), the model reaches \textbf{AUC 0.759} -- clearly separating customers likely to lapse within 90 days from those who will return, and ahead of every rule-based baseline. The 10\% of customers it scores highest churn at \textbf{75.6\%}, against 40.1\% on average, and its calibrated probabilities match observed churn closely (Brier score 0.194).

\section{Key Finding: Risk Is Not the Same as Value}
Despite being the best churn detector, the model is \textbf{not} the most profitable targeting rule at the standard 10\% operating point. Comparing estimated net business impact of contacting the top 10\% of customers each week (40\% offer response rate, 35\% margin, \pounds0.50 contact cost, \pounds5.00 incentive cost):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.6cm}p{2.6cm}p{3.4cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    Targeting rule & Churners caught & Net business impact \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Churn model} & \textbf{1,831} & \textbf{\pounds51,506} \\
    RFM score (hand-built) & 1,732 & \pounds64,616 \\
    Longest time since last purchase & 1,521 & \textbf{\pounds70,214} \\
    Random & 986 & \pounds41,626 \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The model catches far more churners than the simple rules, yet ends up \emph{less} profitable. The reason: a customer's average order value is only weakly related to their churn risk (\pounds269 vs.\ \pounds324 median, churned vs.\ not) -- so ranking purely by churn probability does not automatically prioritize high-value customers. The simpler rules happen to catch a higher-value subset, even while catching fewer churners overall.

\section{Recommendation}
Change the targeting score from ``churn probability'' to \textbf{expected loss}: the model's (calibrated) churn probability $\times$ the customer's order value $\times$ the orders they would place in the next 90 days. We tested this on a realistic weekly campaign -- a fixed weekly budget, each customer contacted at most once every 13 weeks:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
    \toprule
    Ranking (net impact) & 2\% of customers / week & 5\% of customers / week \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Expected loss} & \textbf{\pounds46,366} & \textbf{\pounds65,354} \\
    Longest time since last purchase & \pounds20,120 & \pounds41,195 \\
    Churn probability alone & \pounds12,782 & \pounds37,031 \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Expected loss more than doubles net impact at a 2\% weekly budget, and stays ahead under every way of valuing a customer we tried. Keep the weekly budget small: at 10\% a week, most customers are contacted within a quarter and the ranking barely matters. Validate with a real pilot against a held-out control group before scaling: this dataset contains no past campaign data, so every pound figure here is a scenario estimate under the stated assumptions, not an observed outcome.
