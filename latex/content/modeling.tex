\section{Modeling Objective}
\label{sec:8-1}

Estimate $P(\text{Churn} = 1 \mid \text{Customer History at Snapshot})$ on the final modeling dataset built in Chapter~\ref{ch:data-preparation} (\texttt{modeling\_dataset\_h90.parquet}). Per the Section~\ref{sec:7-9} split table, \textbf{Train} is \texttt{in\_training\_sample} rows within \texttt{split=="train"} (6,453 rows, 46.7\% churn); \textbf{Validation} is every row in \texttt{split=="validation"} (21,514 rows, 52.9\% churn); \textbf{Test} is every row in \texttt{split=="test"} (24,252 rows, 40.1\% churn). The three periods have noticeably different churn base rates -- a direct consequence of the seasonality already documented in Section~\ref{sec:6-14}.

$C = 0.01$ (the strongest regularization tried) wins on PR-AUC -- consistent with Section~\ref{sec:8-4}/\ref{sec:8-5}'s finding that the extra features mostly add noise for this training-set size; heavier regularization suppresses that noise.

After selection, the Training Population is \textbf{re-derived over train + validation}: the eligibility-episode rule (Section~\ref{sec:3-9}) is re-applied to every active row between the first train date and the last validation date (not simply the union of the two periods' original flags), giving 12,019 training rows -- nearly double the 6,453 used so far, since the cooldown restarts over a longer window. Retraining $C=0.01$ Logistic Regression on this pool and evaluating \textbf{once} on test:

\section{Model Selection}
\label{sec:8-8}

The final model is \textbf{Logistic Regression, Full feature set, $C = 0.01$}, refit on the train+validation Training Population (12,019 rows) and evaluated once on test. It was selected over Random Forest and XGBoost on validation PR-AUC (Section~\ref{sec:8-6}) despite neither tree ensemble improving on it -- with only a few thousand training rows, the added model capacity did not pay off. The RFM-only feature set (Section~\ref{sec:8-3}) was statistically indistinguishable from the Full set, but Full was retained for the final model since it is evaluated here under the stronger regularization ($C=0.01$) found in tuning, which absorbs the extra features' noise rather than requiring they be dropped by hand. On test, the final model reaches \textbf{Lift@10\% $= 1.93$} and \textbf{Gain@10\% $= 0.193$} -- targeting the top 10\% of ranked customers each week would reach churners at nearly twice the rate of untargeted outreach, capturing 19.3\% of all churners in that 10\% of the population.

\section{Model Interpretability}
\label{sec:8-9}

SHAP values for the final refit model (\texttt{LinearExplainer}, computed on a sample of 1,000 test rows):

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/ch8_shap_summary.png}
\end{center}

By mean absolute SHAP value, the most influential features are \texttt{frequency\_so\_far} and \texttt{monetary\_so\_far} (the two RFM features driving most of the model's separating power, consistent with Sections~\ref{sec:8-3}--\ref{sec:8-5}), followed by the mean inter-purchase gap, cancellation count, and recent activity features. The directions match intuition: higher frequency and monetary value push the prediction toward \emph{not} churning; a longer typical gap between purchases, more cancellations, and low recent activity all push toward churning. \texttt{recency\_to\_typical\_gap} shows the widest spread for its most extreme values (customers far more overdue than their own normal rhythm), the clearest individual red flag in the tail. \textbf{These are associations the model has learned, not causal effects} (Section~\ref{sec:3-14}) -- a high cancellation count predicting churn, for instance, does not mean cancellations cause churn.

\section{Training Population Ablation}
\label{sec:8-10}

Compare two training schemes for the selected model (Logistic Regression, Full features, $C=0.01$), both trained on the \textbf{train period only} (not the extended refit pool from Section~\ref{sec:8-7}) and evaluated on the \textbf{same test set}:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.5cm}p{9.6cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    Scheme & Training rows \\
    \midrule
    A -- Episode (main) & \texttt{in\_training\_sample} rows, unweighted (6,453 rows) \\
    B -- All weekly, weighted & All weekly active rows in the training period, weighted by \texttt{sample\_weight} (61,415 rows) \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.2cm}p{1.8cm}p{1.8cm}p{2.4cm}p{1.8cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    Scheme & ROC-AUC & PR-AUC & Precision@10\% & Lift@10\% \\
    \midrule
    A -- Episode & 0.750 & 0.631 & 0.716 & 1.787 \\
    B -- Weighted all & 0.755 & 0.649 & 0.766 & 1.912 \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{B is clearly better} on every metric, using $9.5\times$ more rows (61,415 vs.\ 6,453). The episode rule still serves its purpose -- cutting near-duplicate rows and training time by over 90\% (Section~\ref{sec:6-15}) -- but for this dataset's size, that reduction costs some predictive quality: with so few customers and snapshots to begin with, discarding the correlated-but-informative extra rows removes more signal than it removes redundancy. For production use at this data volume, \textbf{Scheme B (all weekly active rows, weighted)} is the better default; the episode scheme remains attractive mainly as the data volume grows and training cost becomes the binding constraint.
