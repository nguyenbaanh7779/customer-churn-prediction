\section{Data Cleaning}
\label{sec:7-1}

Clean transaction records using documented, reproducible rules. Every rule reports how many rows and how much revenue it removes. Applying the full valid-purchase definition below to the raw dataset keeps \textbf{776,577 of 1,067,371 rows (72.8\%)} -- the per-rule breakdown (missing Customer ID 22.8\%, duplicates 3.2\%, invalid quantity/price 2.4\%, cancellations 1.8\%, non-product codes 0.6\%) was already measured in Section~\ref{sec:6-4} and is not repeated here; this chapter reuses the same \texttt{is\_valid} filter to build everything that follows.

\section{Valid Transaction Definition}
\label{sec:7-2}

A \textbf{valid purchase} row must satisfy all of the following:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.8cm}p{10.3cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    Rule & Treatment \\
    \midrule
    Customer ID present & Rows without Customer ID are excluded from customer history (still counted in the Section~\ref{sec:6-3} overview). \\
    Not a cancellation & Invoices starting with "C" are excluded from purchases; kept separately for cancellation features. \\
    Quantity $>$ 0 and Price $>$ 0 & Otherwise excluded. \\
    Product stock code & Non-product codes (e.g.\ postage, manual adjustments, bank charges, marketplace fees) are excluded; the final list is documented. \\
    Exact duplicates & Removed. \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A \textbf{purchase occasion} is one distinct invoice. Frequency and inter-purchase intervals are computed on purchase occasions, not rows: applying this definition to the 776,577 valid rows yields \textbf{36,594 occasions across 5,852 customers}, each occasion carrying its aggregated revenue, quantity and the distinct set of products bought (used by the product-behavior feature in Section~\ref{sec:7-6}).

\section{Customer History Construction}
\label{sec:7-3}

Aggregate valid transaction history by customer while preserving timestamps and product information. The resulting history spans \textbf{2009-12-01 to 2011-12-09} ($\approx$2 years) for all 5,852 customers -- the same span and customer count already established in Chapter~\ref{ch:data-understanding}.

\section{Eligibility Construction}
\label{sec:7-4}

Eligibility is built in two steps.

\textbf{Step 1 -- Weekly active-customer base.} At each weekly date $t$, a customer is active if:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item at least one valid purchase exists on or before $t$ (existing customer);
    \item Recency at $t$ $\leq R_{\max}$ (activity condition);
    \item $t$ lies within the snapshot period (after warm-up; $t + H \leq$ data end for labeled rows).
\end{enumerate}

With $R_{\max} = 136.0$ days (the rounded P90 value from Section~\ref{sec:6-10}, used as the frozen configuration constant for this chapter) and a 6-month warm-up, this produces \textbf{208,764 weekly active-customer base rows}. This is 13 rows above the 208,751 reported in Section~\ref{sec:6-15}, entirely explained by rounding: the exploratory analysis there compared recency against the unrounded quantile (135.97 days), while this chapter uses the rounded $R_{\max} = 136.0$ days as the frozen pipeline configuration -- a handful of rows sit exactly at that boundary.

\textbf{Step 2 -- Population flags on the base table.}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.6cm}p{9.5cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    Flag & Rule \\
    \midrule
    \texttt{in\_training\_sample} & Row selected by the eligibility-episode rule (Section~\ref{sec:3-9}). Computed per customer in date order. \\
    \texttt{in\_scoring\_population} & Row is active and the customer is not under suppression at $t$. In the historical data there are no real campaigns, so suppression is empty and every active row is in the Scoring Population (see Section~\ref{sec:9-1}). \\
    \texttt{sample\_weight} & $1 /$ (number of weekly active rows of the customer in the training period); used only by the ablation scheme (Section~\ref{sec:8-10}). \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Applying the episode-cooldown rule ($C = 90$ days) to the 208,764 active rows selects \textbf{20,152 rows (9.7\%) as \texttt{in\_training\_sample}}; \texttt{in\_scoring\_population} is \texttt{True} for all 208,764 active rows (every active row is scored, since suppression is empty). \texttt{sample\_weight} is left unset here and filled in once the training period is known, in Section~\ref{sec:7-9}.

\section{Customer Snapshot Construction}
\label{sec:7-5}

Construct \textbf{one weekly active-customer base table} of Customer $\times$ Snapshot Date rows, then compute features and labels once for this table. The Training Population and the Scoring Population are filters on it (via the flags above), which guarantees that both use identical feature and label definitions. A direct check confirms every \texttt{in\_training\_sample} row is also an \texttt{in\_scoring\_population} row (the episode rule only subsets the active base, never adds to it) -- the base table has shape $(208{,}764,\ 8)$ at this point, before feature engineering.

\section{Feature Engineering}
\label{sec:7-6}

Generate historical features using information available up to the snapshot date only, via a single backward as-of-merge primitive applied to cumulative per-occasion statistics -- the same mechanism used for recency itself, so every feature is leakage-safe by construction (verified explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:7-8}). A representative subset of each group from the Feature Engineering Strategy (Section~\ref{sec:3-11}) is implemented:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.6cm}p{2.3cm}p{2.3cm}p{3.2cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    Feature & Mean & Median & Max \\
    \midrule
    Recency (days) & 50.5 & 41.6 & 136.0 \\
    Frequency so far & 6.56 & 4 & 367 \\
    Monetary so far (GBP) & 3,152.65 & 1,105.74 & 569,501.50 \\
    Avg.\ order value so far (GBP) & 393.39 & 302.53 & 25,784.32 \\
    Tenure (days) & 277.9 & 248.8 & 733.9 \\
    Mean inter-purchase gap so far (days) & 76.9 & 56.0 & 714.2 \\
    Recency / typical gap & 300.0 & 0.63 & 195,604 \\
    Activity in last 90 days (occasions) & 4.17 & 1 & 361 \\
    Distinct products so far & 86.2 & 53 & 2,515 \\
    Cancellations so far & 1.49 & 0 & 112 \\
    Cancellation value share so far & 0.022 & 0.000 & 0.896 \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Note.} \textbf{A known instability.} \texttt{recency / typical gap} is undefined for the 43,970 rows where the customer has only one occasion so far (no prior gap exists) and is set to \texttt{NaN}. A further 31 rows had a \texttt{mean\_gap\_so\_far} of exactly zero (customers with multiple invoices at the same instant), which would otherwise divide to \texttt{inf} and break downstream modeling in Chapter~\ref{ch:modeling} -- these are also set to \texttt{NaN} rather than \texttt{inf}. Even after this fix the ratio's long tail is extreme (max 195,604, against a median of 0.63) because it divides by a very small but non-zero typical gap; a stricter pipeline would additionally winsorize this feature before modeling.

\section{Churn Label Construction}
\label{sec:7-7}

Use only the prediction window $(t,\, t+H]$ to determine churn, applying the same valid-purchase definition as Section~\ref{sec:7-2}. At $H = 90$ days, \textbf{172,561 of the 208,764 base rows (82.7\%) are labelable} (their full prediction window is observed before the data end); the final, non-provisional churn rate on those rows is \textbf{48.0\%} -- matching the provisional estimate from Section~\ref{sec:6-14} exactly.

\section{Leakage Prevention}
\label{sec:7-8}

No future information may be used in feature construction. The prediction window is reserved for label construction. Aggregates that depend on the whole dataset (scalers, encoders, imputation values, percentile thresholds) are fitted on the training period only, once modeling starts in Chapter~\ref{ch:modeling}. A direct check re-derives one feature (\texttt{frequency\_so\_far}) for every base row and asserts its source timestamp never exceeds the row's snapshot date; this passes for all 208,764 rows, confirming the as-of-merge construction is leakage-safe by result, not only by design.

\textbf{Caveat.} $R_{\max}$ and $H$ themselves were chosen from inter-purchase gaps measured over the \emph{full} dataset in Chapter~\ref{ch:data-understanding}, not the training period alone. A stricter pipeline would re-derive these thresholds from training-period data only; here they are treated as fixed configuration, consistent with their role as project-level design choices rather than learned parameters.

\section{Temporal Dataset Split with Embargo}
\label{sec:7-9}

Split chronologically by snapshot date into train, validation and test, with an \textbf{embargo of $H$ days} between consecutive periods:
\begin{align*}
    \text{first validation date} &\geq \text{last training date} + H \\
    \text{first test date} &\geq \text{last validation date} + H
\end{align*}
Without the embargo, labels of late training snapshots are observed during the validation period, so the model effectively learns from a period it is later evaluated on.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.6cm}p{6.4cm}p{5cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    Split & Rows used & Purpose \\
    \midrule
    Train & \texttt{in\_training\_sample} rows in the train period & Fit candidate models \\
    Validation & \texttt{in\_scoring\_population} rows in the validation period & Tuning, model selection, calibration fit \\
    Final refit & \texttt{in\_training\_sample} rows re-derived over train + validation & Fit the selected model \\
    Test & \texttt{in\_scoring\_population} rows in the test period & Final, operation-like evaluation \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Validation and test therefore measure performance exactly as the model would be used: ranking all active customers each week.

\textbf{Computed timeline ($H = 90$ days, weekly Mondays, 6-month warm-up).} Validation and test are sized to fixed operational targets (8 and 10 weeks) and taken from the end of the 66 labelable weekly dates working backward, with an embargo before each; train absorbs the remaining weeks. This is no longer the illustrative example once sketched for this section -- it is the actual computed split, and it reproduces that example's dates exactly:

The resulting churn rate differs by split -- a reminder that the seasonality already seen in Section~\ref{sec:6-14} shifts the base rate between periods, not just between individual weeks:

\texttt{sample\_weight} is now computed for the 61,415 train-period active rows as $1 /$ (that customer's row count within the train period): it ranges from 0.0417 (a customer active in all 24 train weeks) to 1.0 (a customer active in only one), with a mean of 0.0636 -- confirming the weighting scheme gives every customer roughly equal total weight regardless of how many weekly rows they contributed, as intended for the ablation comparison in Section~\ref{sec:8-10}.

\section{Final Modeling Dataset}
\label{sec:7-10}

Customer ID, snapshot date, segment flag (one-time / repeat), population flags (\texttt{in\_training\_sample}, \texttt{in\_scoring\_population}), sample weight, historical features, churn label and split assignment, produced by reproducible transformations from a single configuration. The final table has \textbf{208,764 rows and 20 columns}: 164,794 rows (79.0\%) are \texttt{repeat}-segment and 43,970 (21.0\%) are \texttt{one-time}-segment at their respective snapshot dates. The table is saved to \texttt{data/processed/}\allowbreak\texttt{modeling\_dataset\_h90.parquet} ($\approx$6.5\,MB) for direct reuse in Chapter~\ref{ch:modeling}.
