This chapter builds the weekly churn \textbf{risk estimate}: a model of the probability that a customer churns given their history at the snapshot date, fitted on the modeling dataset of Chapter~\ref{ch:data-preparation} and calibrated and grouped into ten fixed risk bands. Per the split table of Section~\ref{sec:5-6}, \textbf{train} holds 61,415 active rows of 3,905 customers (43.0\% churn), \textbf{validation} 21,514 rows (52.9\%) and \textbf{test} 24,252 rows (40.1\%); the base rates differ because of the seasonality documented in Section~\ref{sec:5-4}. Every modeling choice is made on validation by a rule fixed in advance (Sections~\ref{sec:6-select}--\ref{sec:6-final}), and the final model is then evaluated once on test (Section~\ref{sec:6-quality}). Whether targeting by the score pays off is a business question, left to Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

\section{Model Selection}
\label{sec:6-select}

Choices are made in a fixed order -- training rows, then features, then algorithm. Where two candidates are close, the gap between them is bootstrapped by resampling whole customers (500 resamples, since a customer's weekly rows are correlated), and the simpler candidate is kept unless the 95\% interval of the gap excludes 0. The gap is measured on validation PR-AUC (full comparison in \texttt{notebooks/04\_modeling.ipynb}); the table reports AUC and the Brier score of calibrated probabilities (Section~\ref{sec:6-quality}), each candidate calibrated on its own training rows so weighted and unweighted fits compare fairly.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training rows.} Scheme B keeps every weekly row and weights each customer equally (Section~\ref{sec:5-3}). Its AUC gain over the episode sample is $+0.007$ (95\% CI $[+0.005, +0.010]$): with only a few thousand customers, discarding 90\% of the rows removes more signal than redundancy. Once both are calibrated, it is also the better-calibrated scheme (Brier 0.214 vs.\ 0.226).
    \item \textbf{Features.} RFM alone captures nearly all of the separable signal; Extended adds purchase rhythm (mean gap, recency relative to it, activity in the last 90 days), a small gain whose interval only just excludes 0. Product and cancellation features (Full) add nothing.
    \item \textbf{Algorithm.} The weekly rows add correlated views of the same customers rather than new ones, and the relationships are close to monotonic, so the tree ensembles find no reliable non-linear splits.
\end{itemize}

All validation Brier scores sit above 0.21 partly because the validation period churns more (52.9\%) than the training period (43.0\%), a seasonal shift no calibration fitted on training data can anticipate.

\section{Hyperparameter Tuning and Final Model}
\label{sec:6-final}

The regularization strength $C$ of the Logistic Regression is tuned on validation (fit on train, compare on validation; a cross-validated search would break the temporal structure):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{1.8cm}p{1.8cm}p{1.8cm}p{6cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    $C$ & AUC & Brier & Tied with the best $C$? \\
    \midrule
    0.001 & 0.775 & 0.213 & No \\
    0.003 & 0.777 & 0.210 & No \\
    \textbf{0.01} & \textbf{0.780} & \textbf{0.210} & \textbf{Yes -- strongest tied regularization} \\
    0.1 & 0.781 & 0.213 & Yes \\
    100 & 0.781 & 0.214 & Best \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Separation is flat above $C = 0.01$, but the coefficients are not: frequency, monetary value and average order value are strongly correlated ($\log$ monetary $\approx$ $\log$ frequency $+$ $\log$ order value), and with weak regularization they trade off -- at $C = 100$ the frequency coefficient turns positive. The parsimony rule therefore takes the \textbf{smallest $C$ statistically tied with the best}, $C = 0.01$, where every coefficient keeps its intuitive sign; it is also better calibrated than the weakly regularized fits.

The final model is \textbf{Logistic Regression, Extended feature set, $C = 0.01$, trained on every weekly active row with per-customer weight $1/n_i$}, refit on every labelable row from the first train date to the last validation date (120,376 rows of 4,571 customers).

\section{Model Evaluation}
\label{sec:6-quality}

The final model is scored once on the 10 test weeks; nothing here feeds back into a choice.

\textbf{Calibration.} Weighting each customer equally lifts the effective churn rate of the training rows to 62.0\% (50.0\% unweighted), so the raw probabilities overestimate risk on unweighted rows: on the refit pool itself they average 55.1\% against a churn rate of 50.0\%. A single log-odds offset $\delta$ is therefore fitted on the refit pool so that the mean calibrated probability equals its churn rate, $\text{logit}_{\text{cal}} = \text{logit}_{\text{raw}} - \delta$ with $\delta = 0.259$. The textbook prior correction $\text{logit}(0.620) - \text{logit}(0.500) = 0.489$ would overshoot (mean 45.4\% on the same rows), because the weights change the mix of customers, not only the base rate. The offset uses training data only and is monotonic -- AUC and every ranking are unchanged -- and the calibrated probability is what the Brier score below and Expected Loss (Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}) use.

\textbf{Risk bands.} Ten bands with fixed cut-offs at the validation-period deciles of the calibrated probability, so each band holds 10\% of validation customers and band membership is comparable across weeks.

Each scoring week is evaluated on four checks: \textbf{AUC} (separation), \textbf{Brier score} (calibrated probability vs.\ observed churn), \textbf{HHI} $= \sum_b s_b^2$ (concentration of customers across the ten bands; 0.10 when spread evenly, 1 when all in one band) and \textbf{CSI} $= \sum_b (s_b - r_b)\ln(s_b / r_b)$ (stability of the band shares $s_b$ against the validation shares $r_b$; below 0.10 stable, 0.10--0.25 moderate shift, above 0.25 major shift).

Separation holds up out of sample: weekly AUC stays between 0.744 and 0.773 (0.780 on validation), and the Brier score stays at 0.192--0.197. HHI stays close to its 0.10 floor, so the model keeps spreading customers across all ten bands rather than piling them into a few. CSI, however, \textbf{rises steadily}, from 0.084 to 0.138, and stays above the 0.10 ``moderate shift'' line from 2011-08-08: as the test period's churn rate falls (44.4\% to 34.8\%), more customers move into the low-risk bands. AUC drifts down with it. Neither is a failure, but together they are the signal the monitoring of Section~\ref{sec:8-6} is designed to catch, and a reason to refit on more recent data.

\textbf{Interpretability} (SHAP, 1,000 test rows). \texttt{frequency\_so\_far} and \texttt{monetary\_so\_far} matter most, followed by \texttt{recency\_days}, the mean inter-purchase gap and \texttt{activity\_last\_90d}. Every direction is intuitive: more purchases, more spend and recent activity push toward \emph{not} churning; a long time since the last purchase and a long typical gap push toward churning. \texttt{recency\_to\_typical\_gap} is the clearest red flag in the tail (customers far more overdue than their own rhythm), while average order value and tenure add little once the others are known. These are associations the model has learned, not causal effects.
